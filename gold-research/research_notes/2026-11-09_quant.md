## 1. Time-series momentum / trend following (gold in sample)

### 1a. Moskowitz, Ooi & Pedersen (2012), "Time Series Momentum", *Journal of Financial Economics* 104(2), 228–250 — SNIPPET
- **URL used:** https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2089463 (abstract); econpapers RePEc:eee:jfinec:v:104:y:2012:i:2:p:228-250.
- **Data:** 58 liquid futures/forwards (equity indices, currencies, commodities, bonds), 1965–2009; main TSMOM tests on 1985–2009. Gold (COMEX) is one of the 58 instruments (gold row visible in snippets with a Jan-1985 start).
- **Rule (reproducible):** at month-end, signal = sign of the instrument's excess return over the prior 12 months; hold 1 month; position size = 40% / ex-ante annualised volatility, where volatility is an exponentially weighted estimate of squared daily returns with a 60-day centre of mass (×261 days). Diversified portfolio = equal-weighted average of the per-instrument volatility-scaled positions.
- **Performance:** every one of the 58 instruments shows a positive 12-month TSMOM t-statistic (per-instrument figure in paper); diversified portfolio gross Sharpe ≈1.0 (secondary summaries range 1.0–1.3); single-asset Sharpes typically 0.3–0.5. Gold-specific Sharpe/t-stat: *could not verify* (one Quantopian replication reports gold ≈ −0.16 Sharpe with a different construction — not the paper's number). Returns are **gross** of commissions, spreads, slippage.
- **OOS/replication:** persistence 1–12 months, partial reversal beyond 12 months (paper). Later replications (Huang et al. "Time-series momentum: is it there?") argue pooled significance is weaker when scaled differently; Hurst et al. extends in-sample back to 1880 (below). Post-2010 commodity trend performance was weak (see 1c).
- **Framework layer:** daily-updated 12-month (or blended 1/3/12-month) sign of gold's excess return as a **directional bias filter**; vol-scaled size as **position sizing**. Caveat: monthly-horizon signal; an intraday system gets at most a slowly-varying bias, and gold's standalone edge is far weaker than the diversified number.

### 1b. Hurst, Ooi & Pedersen (2017), "A Century of Evidence on Trend-Following Investing", *Journal of Portfolio Management* 44(1), 15–29 (working paper 2012/2014 covering 1880–2013) — SNIPPET
- **URL used:** https://www.aqr.com/Insights/Research/Journal-Article/A-Century-of-Evidence-on-Trend-Following-Investing ; mirror https://fairmodel.econ.yale.edu/ec439/hurst.pdf (both unreachable; snippets only).
- **Data:** 67 markets — 29 commodities, 11 equity indices, 15 bonds, 12 currency pairs — monthly, Jan 1880–Dec 2013 (2017 version to 2016). Gold is among the 29 commodities (gold-specific numbers: *could not verify*).
- **Rule:** equal-weighted combination of 1-, 3- and 12-month time-series momentum (long if trailing excess return >0, short if <0), positions volatility-scaled using a covariance matrix from rolling 3-year monthly returns, portfolio targeted at ~10% annualised vol (10% target from memory; not confirmed in snippets), monthly rebalance.
- **Performance:** full sample 1880–2013: 14.9% return, 11.2% vol, Sharpe 0.77 (snippet table; 2017 version reports net Sharpe ≈0.76). Positive in every decade; lowest decade Sharpe 0.27 (1880s). Results shown gross and **net of simulated transaction costs and 2%/20% fees**.
- **OOS:** by construction the 1880–1984 sample is out-of-sample relative to MOP (2012); 2010–2016 extension was positive but the weakest stretch.
- **Framework layer:** same as 1a (bias filter + vol sizing), with stronger prior that the 3-lookback blend is robust. Caveat: portfolio-level evidence; no gold-only Sharpe published.

### 1c. Clare, Seaton, Smith & Thomas (2014), "Trend following, risk parity and momentum in commodity futures", *International Review of Financial Analysis* 31, 1–12 — SNIPPET
- **URL used:** https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/17846/ (unreachable); https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/finana/v31y2014icp1-12.html
- **Data:** ~28 commodity futures (gold included — from memory, *could not verify*), 1992–2011 per Quantpedia summary.
- **Rule:** trend filter = hold a commodity only when its month-end price > its 10-month simple moving average (else cash/T-bills); momentum = rank on trailing 12-month return and hold top N; risk parity = inverse-vol weights. Monthly rebalance.
- **Performance:** best combination gives 15.94% average excess return, Sharpe 0.82; most strategies Sharpe 0.5–0.8; trend-following overlay matters more than the momentum or risk-parity step. Costs: *could not verify*.
- **OOS:** a QuantConnect replication of the method over Jan 2010–Jan 2020 reports Sharpe −0.13, i.e., the commodity trend edge did not persist in the following decade.
- **Framework layer:** 10-month SMA as **bias filter**; caveat: the failed 2010–2020 replication is a strong warning against relying on it alone.

## 2. Moving-average timing on gold

### 2a. Faber (2007; updates 2009, 2013), "A Quantitative Approach to Tactical Asset Allocation", *Journal of Wealth Management* (Spring 2007), SSRN 962461 — SNIPPET
- **URL used:** https://mebfaber.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/05/SSRN-id962461.pdf (unreachable); https://www.cxoadvisory.com/technical-trading/long-term-outperformance-from-trends-defined-by-moving-averages
- **Data:** five total-return indices incl. S&P GSCI (commodities), 1973–2008, 2013 update 1973–2012. Gold is **not** tested separately — only via GSCI (energy-heavy).
- **Rule:** at month-end, long if price > 10-month SMA, else cash (T-bills). One signal per month.
- **Performance:** five-asset timed portfolio 10.5% vs 9.9% buy-and-hold annualised (1973–2012) with drawdown cut from ~46% to <10% (portfolio, 2006 version). GSCI-only rows: *could not verify*; a secondary summary says below-SMA returns are ~60% lower and vol 30% higher on average but that "commodities are the one exception", i.e., the rule worked least well on GSCI. No transaction costs.
- **OOS:** real-time since 2006 reported as consistent by author; critics flag the 10-month window as potentially data-mined for US equities.
- **Framework layer:** monthly 10-SMA state as a coarse **bias/avoidance** switch; caveat: never tested on gold itself and weakest on commodities.

### 2b. 200-day SMA / golden cross on gold — no peer-reviewed study found
- Only practitioner conditional statistics surfaced: SentimenTrader (SNIPPET, https://www.sentimentrader.com/blog/an-important-window-for-golds-attempted-trend-change) reports gold's annualised return since 1975 ≈12.3% when above its 200-day SMA vs ≈1.9% below (an earlier post: 12.7% vs 1.5%). No Sharpe/drawdown, no costs, not peer-reviewed.
- Golden cross (50/200) on gold: **none found**; all rigorous golden-cross tests surfaced are on equity indices (≈ buy-and-hold return with lower drawdown).
- Szakmary, Shen & Sharma (2010), "Trend-following trading strategies in commodity futures: a re-examination", *Journal of Banking & Finance* 34, 409–426 — from memory; 28 markets, ~48 years, dual-MA and channel-breakout rules profitable after costs in most markets incl. precious metals. Gold-specific numbers: *could not verify*; snippets (unreliable third-party page) claim gold's optimal lookback lengthened to 200–250 days post-2000 and profits declined post-2010.
- **Framework layer:** price vs 200-day SMA as a **bias filter** (long-only above, flat/short-only below). Caveat: conditional-return evidence only; no formal test with costs.

## 3. Erb & Harvey (2013), "The Golden Dilemma", *Financial Analysts Journal* 69(4), 10–42 (NBER WP 18706) — SNIPPET
- **URL used:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w18706 ; https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:taf:ufajxx:v:69:y:2013:i:4:p:10-42
- **Data:** US gold price and CPI, 1975–2012 (futures era); overlapping 10-year windows 1975–2011; long-run real price back to 1800s for context.
- **Findings / rule-like statements:** real gold price = gold / CPI has averaged ≈3.2 since futures began (low 1.46 in Mar-2001; ≈7–8 in 2012; ≈9 by 2025 per Harvey commentary). Regressing subsequent 10-year real gold return on starting real price shows strong mean reversion (when real price is above average, next-decade real return is below average); the 2012 level implied ≈ −10%/yr real for the following decade (secondary summary). Gold as inflation hedge: works only over very long horizons; short-horizon inflation beta/R² is low (exact coefficient *could not verify*). Also tests currency-debasement hedge, safe haven, and "de-dollarisation/EM central bank" scenarios.
- **Performance numbers:** none in trading-strategy form; no costs.
- **OOS:** the 2012 implied −10%/yr real return **did not materialise** — gold rose strongly 2019–2025 with the ratio reaching ≈9; Erb & Harvey revisit in "Is There Still a Golden Dilemma?" (SSRN 4807895, May 2024) asking whether this is a regime shift or another cycle top.
- **Real-yield relationship (quantified source):** Barsky, Epstein, Lafont-Mueller & Yoo (2021), "What Drives Gold Prices?", *Chicago Fed Letter* No. 464 — SNIPPET (https://www.chicagofed.org/publications/chicago-fed-letter/2021/464). Three channels: inflation hedge, expected long-term real rates, "bad times" hedge. Snippets conflict on the elasticity (one quotes −13.1% real gold price per +1pp long-term real rate; a third-party summary says −3.4%) — *could not verify which is correct*; the letter states the strong inverse relationship is absent before 2001. World Gold Council GRAM (gold.org) is a monthly multiple regression with four driver groups (economic expansion, risk & uncertainty, opportunity cost incl. yields and USD, momentum); it is an **attribution/contemporaneous** model, with WGC itself noting the yield sensitivity re-estimated over 5 years is "markedly lower" and that 2022's real-yield surge "failed to drag gold down". A University of Malta thesis (2-century sample) finds the real-rate relationship non-significant post-COVID.
- **Framework layer:** real gold price (gold/CPI z-score) and the change in 10-y TIPS yield as a **regime/bias input**, not a signal. Main caveat: these are contemporaneous or decade-horizon relationships; no daily/weekly predictive power demonstrated, and both broke down 2022–2025.

## 4. Gold seasonality

### 4a. Baur (2013), "The Autumn Effect of Gold", *Research in International Business and Finance* 27(1), 1–11 (SSRN WP "The Seasonality of Gold – Jewelery Demand and Investor Behavior", Jan 2012) — SNIPPET
- **URL used:** https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/riibaf/v27y2013i1p1-11.html ; https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1989593
- **Data:** daily London fixing spot and COMEX gold futures, 1980/1981–2010; gold specifically.
- **Rule:** calendar: September and November are the only months with positive and statistically significant mean returns (unconditionally and after controlling for risk factors). Secondary source: Sept mean ≈ +2.2%, Nov ≈ +1.8% (*could not verify* from paper). Autumn also shows higher volatility. Explanations: hedging ahead of equity "Halloween effect", Indian wedding-season demand, daylight/sentiment.
- **Costs:** none; not a trading strategy per se.
- **OOS / replication:** **Failed to persist.** Potrykus & Augustynowicz (2024), "The 'autumn effect' in the gold market — does it contradict the Adaptive Market Hypothesis?", *International Journal of Management and Economics* 60(3), 157–172 (DOI 10.2478/ijme-2024-0011) — SNIPPET: extended replication; Sept/Nov returns negative from Dec-2010 to Jul-2022; autumn effect "reversed and replaced by the winter effect". A 2020 arXiv decomposition study ("Turn-of-the-Year Affect in Gold Prices", arXiv:2003.11027) finds Nov/Jan/Feb positive and Mar/Jun/Jul negative across currencies — "partial support" for autumn, "strong support" for winter.
- **Framework layer:** at most a mild **seasonal tilt** (long-bias Nov–Feb, caution Mar/Jun/Jul). Caveat: effect reversed OOS; treat as low-confidence prior only.

### 4b. Day-of-week — Yu, Lee & Shih (2016), "Weekday effects on gold: Tokyo, London, and New York markets", *Banks and Bank Systems* 11(2), 33–44 — SNIPPET
- **URL used:** https://doi.org/10.21511/bbs.11(2).2016.04 ; https://core.ac.uk/download/201399031.pdf
- **Data:** gold prices in Tokyo, London, New York, 29 Dec 1978–16 Dec 2011; probability-distribution approach.
- **Findings:** Friday positive and significantly higher, Tuesday negative and significantly lower; Monday effect absent; London has highest mean returns, then NY, then Tokyo. Older: Ball, Torous & Tschoegl (1982) (1975–79, Wednesday high) and Ma (1986), *Journal of Futures Markets* 6(3). A 2020 *Resources Policy* paper finds Mondays raise gold implied vol (GVZ). Magnitudes/t-stats *could not verify*; no costs.
- **Framework layer:** weak **timing tilt** (Fri long-bias, Tue caution). Caveat: minor-journal evidence, sample ends 2011, effect sizes likely inside spreads.

## 5. COT positioning as a gold signal

- **Sanders, Irwin & Merrin (2009), "Smart Money: The Forecasting Ability of CFTC Large Traders in Agricultural Futures Markets", *Journal of Agricultural and Resource Economics* 34(2), 276–296** — SNIPPET (https://ageconsearch.umn.edu/record/54547/files/JARE_Aug09__04R_pp276-296.pdf). 10 agricultural markets (gold **not** in sample), weekly COT 1995–2006 (period from memory). Granger-causality/regressions: very little evidence that any trader group's positions lead returns; substantial evidence positions **follow** prices (non-commercials trend-follow). Conclusion: COT does not support return prediction.
- **Wang (2003), "The behavior and performance of major types of futures traders", *Journal of Futures Markets* 23(1), 1–31** — SNIPPET (https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:wly:jfutmk:v:23:y:2003:i:1:p:1-31; MPRA 36426). 15 US futures markets, COT 1993–2000 (period from memory; whether gold is among the 15: *could not verify*). Speculators are contrarian and respond to sentiment; hedgers are positive-feedback traders with poor performance. Wang's related 2001 work builds a "sentiment index" = percentile rank of net speculator position over 3 years, finding extreme speculator sentiment positively predicts returns in some markets — coefficients for metals *could not verify*.
- **Gold-specific: Mutafoglu, Tokat & Tokat (2012), "Forecasting precious metal price movements using trader positions", *Resources Policy* 37(3), 273–280** — SNIPPET (https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jrpoli/v37y2012i3p273-280.html). Gold, silver, platinum; VAR/Granger. Returns significantly explain all trader groups' positions (structural break early 2000s); non-commercial and non-reportable positions positively correlated with returns; **net positions do not lead returns**, with "some evidence that extreme positions have forecasting ability".
- **Verdict:** in gold, COT net positioning is a lagging/coincident trend-following footprint, not a leading signal; only percentile extremes show weak forecasting value.
- **Framework layer:** 3-year percentile of managed-money net long as a **fade/avoidance filter at extremes** (e.g., >90th percentile → no new longs). Caveat: weekly, 3-day-lagged data; evidence of predictive power is marginal.

## 6. Volatility-breakout / ATR / Donchian systems on gold with published numbers
- **Peer-reviewed gold-specific test with Sharpe/drawdown: none found.** Closest: Szakmary et al. (2010, above) channel-breakout rules across 28 commodities incl. gold (gold row *could not verify*); Covel (2006) 20-market Donchian test cited secondhand.
- Practitioner backtest (SNIPPET, https://algomatictrading.substack.com/p/strategy-8-the-easiest-trend-system): daily Donchian breakout on gold and Nasdaq futures 1990–2025; 2010–2025 window 6.04% CAGR, −16.95% max DD, MAR 0.35 — unclear whether gold alone; no Sharpe; costs unclear. Bollinger-squeeze and ATR-channel tests on GC: **none found**.
- **Framework layer:** none evidence-backed; treat Donchian/ATR as untested hypotheses to backtest in-house.

## 7. DXY and real yields as regime filters (predictive, not contemporaneous)
- **No paper found that demonstrates out-of-sample daily/weekly predictive power of DXY or TIPS yields for gold returns.** Granger-causality studies (mostly VAR on levels, Chinese/other journals, SNIPPET) are mixed: some find DXY → gold unidirectional, one finds neither dollar index nor dollar rates Granger-cause US gold. Chicago Fed (2021) and WGC GRAM quantify **contemporaneous** sensitivities only (see §3); a Fear-and-Greed study notes daily gold return predictability from sentiment/macro variables is essentially nil.
- **Framework layer:** use same-day DXY/TIPS direction only as a **confirmation/avoidance filter** (e.g., don't fade gold when real yields and DXY both move against it). Caveat: no evidence of lead; relationship weakened 2022–2025.

## 8. Intraday gold evidence not already covered

### 8a. Macro-release reaction
- **Elder, Miao & Ramchander (2012), "Impact of macroeconomic news on metal futures", *Journal of Banking & Finance* 36(1), 51–65** — SNIPPET (https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jbfina/v36y2012i1p51-65.html; CSU repository). Intraday COMEX gold, silver, copper, 2002–2008; standardised surprises for US releases. Positive growth surprises push gold (and silver) **down**, copper up; the 8:30 ET releases — **nonfarm payrolls** and durable goods — have the largest effect; response "swift and significant". Magnitudes per 1-SD surprise and drift tests: *could not verify*.
- **Cai, Cheung & Wong (2001), "What moves the gold market?", *Journal of Futures Markets* 21(3), 257–278** — SNIPPET. COMEX gold futures intraday (1990s); 23 US releases; employment report, GDP, CPI and personal income have the largest volatility impact; strong intraday volatility periodicity and long-memory volatility.
- **Smales & Yang (2015), "The importance of belief dispersion in the response of gold futures to macroeconomic announcements", *International Review of Financial Analysis* 41, 292–302** — SNIPPET. COMEX gold tick data: bulk of the price/volume/volatility reaction is complete **within 90 seconds**; larger reaction to good-for-economy (bad-for-gold) news; unemployment rate and GDP surprises largest; reaction scales with analyst-forecast dispersion. Companion Smales, O'Grady & Yang (2015, *Applied Economics Letters*, 30-second data) finds higher volatility, wider spreads, lower volume in the hour around releases.
- **Awartani, Hussain & Virk (2024), "How do the gold intra-day returns and volatility react to monetary policy shocks?", *International Review of Financial Analysis* 95, 103486** — SNIPPET. High-frequency gold around FOMC: returns and volatility 5 minutes after the shock respond more to **easing** surprises than tightening; price and volatility adjustment "continue for longer than five minutes", which the authors call potential short-term inefficiency (i.e., some post-FOMC drift). Magnitudes: *could not verify*. Smales (2014, *JBF* 49, 275–286) adds that negative news sentiment moves gold more than positive (2003–2012).
- **Pre-FOMC drift in gold:** no gold-specific study found (Lucca & Moench is equities).
- **Framework layer:** **avoidance window** ±2 min around 8:30 ET NFP/CPI/durable goods and 14:00 FOMC; **directional rule** after the release: fade-free follow of the surprise sign (good data → short bias, with stronger follow-through after dovish FOMC surprises). Caveat: most adjustment is inside 90 s; drift magnitude post-2015 is unquantified in these sources.

### 8b. London AM/PM auction era (post 20 March 2015)
- **Crain & Hoelscher, "Fixing the Fix for Silver and Gold", Missouri State (BearWorks)** — SNIPPET (https://bearworks.missouristate.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1565&context=articles-cob). Gold/silver futures, pre-period 2 Jan 2008–19 Mar 2015 vs post-auction. **No meaningful differences in returns around the fix after the reform**; structural changes in volatility (gold futures volatility *increased* post-reform; silver implied vol fell). Aspris ("Towards a New Fix") and an EFMA 2017 paper find the silver auction dampened volatility and cut cumulative abnormal returns around the fix.
- **Implication:** the pre-2015 PM-fix downside drift (Caminschi & Heaney) is **not documented as persisting** in the auction era; no study quantifies a post-2015 AM/PM drift in gold.
- **Framework layer:** treat 10:30/15:00 London as **volatility events** only, not directional. Caveat: single working-paper source.

### 8c. Session structure / intraday periodicity
- **Batten, Lucey, McGroarty, Peat & Urquhart (2017), "Stylized facts of intraday precious metals"** (venue *could not verify*; SNIPPET, https://reading-clone.eprints-hosting.org/79175/1/BattenLuceyMcGroartyPeatUrquhart2017.pdf): 15+ years of 5-minute gold/silver/platinum/palladium; "strong evidence of periodicity in returns, volatility, volume and bid-ask spread"; compares London OTC spot vs COMEX.
- **Hauptfleisch, Putniņš & Lucey (2016), "Who Sets the Price of Gold? London or New York", *Journal of Futures Markets* 36(6), 564–586** — SNIPPET: 17 years intraday; NY futures dominate price discovery on average; share shifts with daylight hours, liquidity and macro announcements.
- **Iwatsubo, Watkins & Xu (2018), *Journal of Commodity Markets* 11, 59–71** — SNIPPET: Tokyo gold/platinum futures dominated by uninformed trading; NY has informed trading; intraday seasonality in efficiency/liquidity by Tokyo/London/NY session.
- Asia vs London vs NY **mean-return** comparison post-2015: **none found** (only Yu et al. 2016, to 2011: London highest).
- **Framework layer:** concentrate directional trades in the London/NY overlap where price discovery and volume sit; use Asia as range/setup period. Caveat: evidence is about volatility/liquidity periodicity, not session-return drift.

## Cross-item synthesis for an intraday XAUUSD framework
1. **Bias layer:** 12-month TSMOM sign (±) and/or price vs 10-month SMA / 200-day SMA — the only layer with century-long, cost-inclusive evidence (portfolio level), but weak post-2010 in commodities and never gold-only.
2. **Regime layer:** real gold price z-score and real-yield/DXY same-day direction — contemporaneous only; use for position-size scaling, not entry.
3. **Avoidance windows:** ±2 min around 8:30 ET NFP/CPI/durables and 14:00 FOMC (90-second adjustment); 10:30/15:00 London auctions as volatility, not direction.
4. **Post-event drift:** follow surprise sign after FOMC (asymmetric, dovish stronger) — only qualitatively supported.
5. **Low-confidence tilts:** Nov–Feb seasonal long bias (autumn effect reversed), Friday+/Tuesday− day-of-week, fade COT managed-money extremes (>90th pct).
6. **Not evidence-backed:** Donchian/ATR/Bollinger squeeze on gold; golden cross on gold.
